Good pruning is invisible. The tree looks natural, is lighter in the right places and is less likely to drop limbs in the next storm. Bad pruning, such as topping, flush cuts or stripping the interior, weakens a tree for years. This page covers what to ask for.

## Common reasons to trim

- **Clearance**: keeping branches off the roof, gutters, driveway and sidewalk.
- **Deadwood removal**: dead limbs are the first to fall in wind and ice.
- **Structural pruning**: training young trees to one strong leader so they don't split later.
- **Thinning**: selectively removing branches to reduce wind load without stripping the canopy.

## What good pruning looks like

| Good practice | Red flag |
|---|---|
| Cuts just outside the branch collar | Flush cuts against the trunk |
| No more than about a quarter of the live canopy removed at once | "Topping" the tree to a flat height |
| Climbing without spikes on trees that are staying | Spikes used on a healthy tree that isn't being removed |
| Clear scope: which limbs, why, cleanup included | Vague "trim it back" pricing |

## When to trim

Late winter, while the tree is dormant, suits most structural pruning. You can see the branch structure and avoid peak pest activity. Remove dead or hazardous limbs whenever you find them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is topping a tree ever a good idea?

Arboriculture standards discourage topping. It causes weak, fast-growing sprouts and decay at the cut ends, which often makes the tree more hazardous than before.

### How often should trees be trimmed?

Mature shade trees usually need pruning every few years. Young trees benefit from lighter structural pruning more often. Fast-growing species near structures may need more frequent clearance work.
